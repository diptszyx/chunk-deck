# english — learning English fast, for conversation

Separate topic from `~/project/income`. Goal: listening and speaking for real communication, not
exam grammar. Everything below came from primary research, not from language-learning blogs.

## The principle: cut by the numbers, not by feel

### 1. Vocabulary — the clearest 80/20 there is

| Word group | Coverage it adds |
|---|---|
| First 1,000 words | ~80% of running text |
| First 2,000 | ~83% |
| Words 2,001–4,000 | +5% |
| Words 4,001–6,000 | +3% |

Past 2,000 words, returns collapse. Speaking also needs **less** vocabulary than reading: 98%
coverage of conversation takes 6,000–7,000 word families, written text 8,000–9,000 (Nation 2006).

### 2. Grammar — replaced by chunks, not skipped

Native speakers do not assemble sentences word by word from rules; they reuse prefabricated
**chunks** (Pawley & Syder 1983). One study found chunk use predicted fluency (β = .40, p = .015 —
small sample, n = 38, read as direction not law). The mechanism: a chunk costs no grammar
processing, which frees attention for *what* to say.

So learn `The thing is…`, `Does that make sense?`, `It depends on…` as single units. Do not
analyse the grammar inside them.

### 3. Pronunciation — drop 90%, fix 10%

Munro & Derwing's most robust finding: **accent and intelligibility are partly independent.** A
heavy accent can be fully understood. What actually breaks understanding:

- errors on **high functional load** sounds (sounds whose error changes the word)
- **sentence stress** — misplaced stress damages meaning more than mispronounced sounds

Do not aim to sound native. It costs years and does not change whether you are understood.

### 4. Listening — the bottleneck almost everyone treats wrongly

The problem for learners who read well but listen badly is not missing vocabulary. Field (2008):
learners **know the words but cannot recognise them in connected speech.** Native speech runs at
over six syllables per second, words run together and change shape (`want to` → `wanna`).

More passive listening does not fix this. **Dictation does**: hear a short sentence, write every
word, compare to the original, see where the ear cut it wrong.

### 5. Speaking — the 4/3/2 technique

Nation (1989): tell the same story three times, to three listeners, in **4 min → 3 min → 2 min**.
Speech rate rises and hesitations fall between the first and third telling. The shrinking clock
forces automaticity instead of translating from Vietnamese.

### 6. Memory — spacing has a formula

- **Spaced repetition** (Cepeda 2006: 839 assessments, 317 experiments). Optimal gap ≈ **10–20% of
  how long you need to remember** (Cepeda 2008, 1,350 people). For a 1-year horizon, ~5% → ~18 days.
  Reviewing the same item daily is waste.
- **Retrieval beats re-reading**: g = 0.50 [0.42, 0.58] (Rowland 2014, 159 effect sizes). Stronger
  when retrieval is effortful and followed by feedback. Recall first, then check — never the reverse.

## Cut / keep

| Cut | Keep |
|---|---|
| Perfect continuous tenses, 3rd conditional, inversion, reduced relative clauses | Present simple + continuous, past simple, `will` / `going to` |
| Memorising phrasal-verb lists | 200–300 conversational **chunks**, whole |
| Vocabulary beyond the first ~3,000 words | First 2,000 words (83% coverage) |
| Formal writing, spelling drills | Sentence stress |
| Trying to remove the accent | A few high-functional-load sounds |
| Passive podcast listening | Dictation: hear → write → compare |
| Getting grammar right before speaking | Speak wrong from day one, correct later |

## The honest limit on "fast"

Cambridge's guided learning hours: **B1 → B2 takes 180–260 hours.** No method breaks that. The
point of cutting is not to shrink 180 hours to 20 — it is to spend those 180 hours where the return
is highest, instead of on advanced grammar and rare words, where many people spend 500 hours and
still cannot hold a conversation.
